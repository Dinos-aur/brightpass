# Brightpass privacy policy

*Last updated: 17 October 2026*

**Brightpass** predicts when the International Space Station passes over you.
It is made by Loop.dev, an independent developer in the UK ("we").

It is built to work on your phone alone. There are no accounts, no
advertising, no analytics and no tracking, and **we never receive any data
about you**, unless you email us.

## What the app uses, and where it goes

### Your location

The app needs to know roughly where you are, because pass times depend on it.
You can type the name of a town, or let the app ask your phone for its
**approximate** location. The app never asks for your precise location, and it
never tracks you in the background.

Town names are looked up in a list that comes with the app (towns and cities
from [GeoNames](https://www.geonames.org)), so what you type, and the town the
app names for your location, never leave your phone either.

The location is rounded to about a kilometre and kept on your phone, in the
app's private storage. It is used there to calculate passes and **is never
sent to us.** It leaves your phone only if you turn on cloud forecasts (below),
and then only to MET Norway.

### Orbit data from CelesTrak

To predict passes, the app downloads the ISS's current orbit from
[CelesTrak](https://celestrak.org), a free public service in the United
States, about once a day. The request asks only for the ISS's orbit. It
contains nothing about you or your location. As with any website, CelesTrak's
servers see your IP address when the request arrives.

### Cloud forecasts (only if you turn them on)

Cloud forecasts are off unless you say yes. The app asks once, while you set
it up, with "No thanks" and "Use them" and neither picked for you; carrying
on without answering counts as no. You can change your answer in Settings
at any time. When they are on, the app asks [MET Norway](https://api.met.no), the Norwegian
Meteorological Institute, for the weather forecast where you are: cloud,
fog and humidity, which the app uses to rate each pass. The request
contains your approximate location, rounded to about a kilometre, and the
app's name. It contains nothing else about you. As with any website, MET
Norway's servers see your IP address. The app asks at most about once an
hour while you use it, and about twice a day in the background if you have
alerts on. Turning cloud forecasts
off stops these requests. MET Norway is in Norway, where the GDPR applies,
and its own privacy statement covers what it does with requests.

### Sharing a pass, and your calendar

If you share a pass or add it to your calendar, the app hands its time,
direction and brightness to the app you pick, and only then. What happens
next is up to that app.

### Crash reports

The app has no crash-reporting code. If you have chosen to share usage and
diagnostics data with Google on your phone, Google may tell us through Google
Play Console when the app crashes or freezes: the technical details of the
error, and the phone model and Android version, but not who you are. That is
Google's service, under [Google's privacy policy](https://policies.google.com/privacy).

## Permissions

| Permission | Why |
|---|---|
| Approximate location | Only if you choose "use my location", to find where you are. Asked for when you tap it, with an explanation first. You can type a town instead. |
| Internet | To download orbit data, and cloud forecasts if you turn them on. |
| Camera | For the AR Camera, which shows the camera picture with the ISS marked on it. The picture stays on screen and is never recorded or sent anywhere. |
| Notifications | To remind you before a good pass, if you turn reminders on. Asked for then, not before. |

## Keeping and deleting your data

Everything the app keeps about you (your saved place and your settings) is on your
phone, in the app's private storage, which other apps cannot read. It stays
there until you change it. The app turns off Android's backups and its
copying to a new phone, so none of it goes to your Google account or anywhere
else; on a new phone, you set the app up again.

Uninstalling the app deletes everything it kept, as does clearing the app's
storage in Android's settings.

All the app's network requests use HTTPS, so they are encrypted on the way.

## The law, and your rights

UK and EU data protection law (the UK GDPR and the GDPR) give you rights over
your personal data: to see it, correct it and delete it, and to withdraw your
consent.

- **Cloud forecasts** are the only time anything about you (your approximate
  location) leaves your phone, and they rely on your **consent**. You can
  withdraw it at any time by turning them off in Settings.
- **The orbit download** is needed for the app to work at all. It carries
  nothing about you beyond the IP address that any internet request reveals.
- **We hold nothing about you** (unless you email us, below), so there is
  nothing for us to show, correct or delete: it is all on your phone, under
  your control. For what MET Norway or CelesTrak log when requests reach them,
  ask them.

If you have a concern, please contact us first (below). You can also complain
to the UK's Information Commissioner's Office ([ico.org.uk](https://ico.org.uk)),
or to the data protection authority where you live.

We do not sell data, and the app makes no automated decisions about you.

## If you email us

If you write to us, we get your email address and whatever you send, and use
them only to answer you. The emails are kept in Gmail, Google's email service,
for as long as we need them to deal with what you asked, and then deleted. You
can ask us to delete them sooner.

## Children

The app does not collect personal information from anyone, children included.

## Changes

If the app ever starts sending data anywhere new, this policy will change
first, and the app will ask before doing it. The date at the top shows when it
last changed.
